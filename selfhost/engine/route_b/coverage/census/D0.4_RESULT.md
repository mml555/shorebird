## Against the precommitted gate — the honest reading

The gate had two branches and this result falls between them.

    "top few sole blockers, even 42% -> 15%"   -> open D-TEAROFF
    "collapse, 42.7% -> 1-3%"                  -> implement nothing

Tear-offs came in at **6.67%, three methods**. That is below the branch that
opens the lane and above the branch that closes it. Rather than pick the branch
I prefer, the observation is: **tear-off prevalence tracks how widget-shaped the
app is.** Wonderous's blocked set is almost entirely `build`/`_buildX`;
localsend's three tear-off methods are also all `build`. The construct is real
and its lowering is still cheap — there is just far less of it outside a
showcase app.

The finding that *did* replicate was not on the gate's list: `super`, at 35.92%
and 48.89%, from the same shape in both — `initState` and `dispose` overrides.

## Small-count warning, stated plainly

localsend has **45 blocked methods**. One method is 2.2 percentage points. Its
`6.67%` for tear-offs is three methods and its `48.89%` for super is twenty-two.
Treat the ORDERING as the result and the percentages as coarse; that is what the
precommit said it would do, and it matters more here than it did on Wonderous.

## Two more measurement corrections, in the open

D0.3's lesson repeated twice more. Neither was visible without reading bodies.

**1 — this corpus is 97% generated code.** The first localsend census reported
**19,164** instance procedures from 386 Dart files. Nearly all of it was
codegen: ~55 slang locale files of ~316 translation getters, plus
flutter_rust_bridge bindings. Left in, it dilutes every percentage roughly
tenfold and makes the corpora non-comparable — Wonderous has none at all.

**2 — a filename filter was the wrong instrument.** Excluding `.g.dart` caught
slang but missed `frb_generated.dart` and `rust/api/*.dart`, which are generated,
carry the standard marker, and are committed. With only that filter, localsend
appeared to be dominated by `construct.this_escape` at **42.51%** — and **101 of
those 108 escapes were in the FRB bindings.** A ranking read off that would have
opened a lane on generated code.

The census now records `generated` per row from the **convention**, not a list of
tools: the file head says it was generated and says not to edit it. build_runner
(`GENERATED CODE - DO NOT MODIFY BY HAND`), flutter_rust_bridge (`@generated`)
and slang (`Generated file. Do not edit.`) all satisfy it; a list of filename
patterns would have had to grow once per corpus, which is another way of saying
it is tuned. It under-excludes rather than over-excludes, and the unfiltered view
is kept beside the filtered one (`CENSUS_localsend_unfiltered.txt`).

**The exclusion is applied identically to every corpus.** On Wonderous it removes
zero rows and the report says so — which is the check that it is a filter and not
a thumb on the scale.

## One shape neither corpus predicted

    SwitchStatement   4 occurrences, 4 sole-blocked   (localsend only)

`switch (this)` inside an enum's `get:label`. `this` as a switch scrutinee is not
a receiver access, and is neither an escape into someone else's hands nor a
tear-off. Four methods, so it decides nothing — recorded because raw Kernel node
names are reported precisely so an unexamined shape appears under its own name
instead of inside an `other` row.

## What this licenses, and what it does not

* **D1 stays rejected, now on two corpora.** Compound same-offset is 1.94% and
  2.22%; receiver-in-closure work is 0.97% and 13.33% — and localsend's
  `this_escape`, once generated code is out, is six methods, mostly
  `WidgetsBinding.instance.addObserver(this)`.
* **`super` is the replicated finding.** Per the standing ruling it is still
  priced separately: a second-corpus win licenses a **design investigation**, not
  an implementation lane. It cannot become `self.foo()` without changing
  dispatch, and `ROUTE_B.md` already states what it needs — the Kernel-resolved
  superclass target driving a representation the payload does not have.
* **D-TEAROFF is not opened on this evidence.** It replicated weakly. It also
  did not vanish, and it remains the cheapest-looking mechanism on the board, so
  it stays a candidate rather than being closed.
* **Two apps are not a distribution.** Both are open-source consumer apps built
  by small teams. Neither is a large commercial codebase, which is where patch
  demand actually lives — and demand is still the question P1.5 left open and
  that none of D0 answers.
